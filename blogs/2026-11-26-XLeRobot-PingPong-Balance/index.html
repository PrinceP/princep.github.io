Two robot arms balancing ping-pong balls, eyes on their own wrists

Both SO-101 arms of my XLeRobot hold a table-tennis paddle and keep a ping-pong ball balanced on it, at the same time, for about four minutes. Each arm sees its ball only through its own wrist camera. The controller is zecice's PPO policy, trained in MuJoCo for a single SO-101 and used here without retraining. The work was getting a two-arm mobile robot, a white ball and different cameras to look enough like that simulation.

How it works

The camera is the only sensor for the ball. Everything else the controller knows comes from the arm's own joint readings. Each arm runs this loop in its own process, 30 times a second:

The paddle is clamped edge-on to the wrist camera. The camera sees the blade from the side, as a line, and the ball close to one image column. Its height in the image and its size tell the estimator where the ball sits along and across the blade.

Why a sim-trained controller works on a real arm

zecice's main idea: train the controller with the real system's flaws from the start. Then nothing about the real arm surprises it.

  • Latency. Each training episode gets a random control delay of 0–4 frames (0–133 ms), and the last 3 actions are in the observation, so the policy knows what's still in flight.
  • Soft servos. Servo gains are low and randomized, because cheap servos sag and lag.
  • Realistic ball state. The ball position the controller sees is noisy like the estimator's output, and its velocity comes from the same finite-difference filter the robot uses. A controller trained on exact velocity failed completely on velocity from the camera.

What didn't work for zecice: an end-to-end CNN policy on wrist images. It was fine in simulation but barely saw the real ball. A plain colour detector plus a tiny estimator replaced it.

In randomized simulation, 97.5% of episodes balance for 10 s, and 113 of 120 with the full camera pipeline simulated. Training takes about 2 hours and 29 million steps.

What had to change for the XLeRobot

The policy stayed the same. Everything around it had to change, in roughly this order.

1. Calibration

  • Calibrate in the middle pose. My first XLeRobot calibration was done with the arms folded at rest. That gave shoulder_lift and elbow_flex the whole encoder circle (0–4095), so the usable range ended at the rest pose. Recalibrating with the upper arm up, forearm forward and wrist straight fixed it.
  • One file per arm. XLeRobot keeps one combined calibration file. The balancer uses LeRobot's single-arm SOFollower, so hardware.xlerobot split-calib copies the values unchanged into one file per arm. They match what's in the motors, so no recalibration prompt appears.

2. Wrist cameras

  • All three Innomaker cameras report the same serial number, so they're selected by USB port (/dev/v4l/by-path), not by ID or /dev/videoN.
  • They have no 320×240 mode. OpenCV silently falls back to 640×360 (16:9), which would stretch the 4:3 image the estimator was trained on. 640×480 MJPG keeps the shape.
  • A capture buffer of 1 halves the frame rate to 15 fps on these cameras. A buffer of 2 gives 29.6 fps on both wrist cameras at once.
  • Auto exposure's dynamic frame rate is off, so a dim room can't slow the loop down.

With the paddle clamped edge-on, the real view matches zecice's simulated camera closely: the blade near the image centre and the resting ball at about x +0.2, y −0.2.

3. A white ball instead of an orange one

The detector looks for orange. My balls are white. A "white" threshold (low saturation, high brightness) also matches white walls, the wooden handle, the printed arm parts and skin. Three things made it reliable:

  • Region of interest. Only the strip just past the blade line and above the handle is searched, where a ball on the blade can actually be.
  • Saturation up to 120. The red rubber tints the ball pink (you can see it in the video). With a strict threshold, only a crescent of the ball was found, its size shrank, and the ball was "lost" while sitting at the centre. Most short early attempts ended that way.
  • Size calibration (--r-corr). The estimator turns blob size into position along the blade: 5–10% of size is about 1 cm. With the ball resting at the centre, --r-corr is set so the estimate reads (0, 0): 1.15 on the left, 1.2 on the right.

An orange ball would have avoided all of section 3.

4. Paddle grip and home pose

  • The wrist has to be lifted about 90° before the paddle goes in, because the XLeRobot's cart is in the way at rest. The handle is clamped deep in the jaws, blade edge-on to the camera.
  • Level the home pose with the ball, not by eye. Posed by eye, both blades were 9–10° off level, and the policy has no integral term to correct a tilt. So: place the ball, nudge wrist_flex and wrist_roll 1–2° at a time with hardware.jog --rel, and save when the ball rests.
  • Do that with the paddle clamped exactly as it will run. Re-clamping, or an extra squeeze, tips a round handle in the jaws by a degree or two. Runs start with --grip-load 0 for that reason.

5. Servos

6. Two arms at once

  • One torch thread per process. Each arm runs in its own process (scripts/run_both.sh). By default each process ran about 20 busy PyTorch threads, and two of them starved each other. With one thread each, both keep 30 Hz.
  • Start only on a resting ball. Starting while the hand placing the ball was still in view gave a wrong first estimate, a jerk, and a ball thrown toward the tip. Now control starts only once the ball has rested near the centre for 0.5 s.
  • Keep the goals the arm already holds. On start, run_real keeps the arm's current goals (with sag compensation) instead of re-sending home, so it doesn't droop and roll a resting ball off.

Run it

On the robot computer, with the XLeRobot host or bridge stopped (these scripts need the arms' serial ports). Every hardware script takes --arm left|right, which picks the port, calibration, wrist camera and home file.

python -m hardware.xlerobot split-calib                       # once per calibration: one SOFollower file per arm
python -m hardware.jog --arm right wrist_flex=-12             # lift the wrist clear of the cart
python -m hardware.jog --arm right wrist_roll=0 gripper=40    # centre the roll, open the gripper
python -m hardware.run_real --arm right --grip-only --home /nonexistent  # o, insert the paddle edge-on, Enter: squeeze
python -m hardware.pose_guide --arm right                     # arm goes limp: pose it with the paddle level, Ctrl+C saves home
python -m hardware.jog --arm right --rel wrist_flex=-1        # nudge until the ball rests, then --save-home
python -m hardware.run_real --arm right --ball white --kp 64 --r-corr 1.2 --grip-load 0   # balance
python -m hardware.torque_off --arm right                     # hold the arm: it goes limp

# both arms at once
LEFT_ARGS="--r-corr 1.15" RIGHT_ARGS="--r-corr 1.2" scripts/run_both.sh --ball white --kp 64 --grip-load 0

Checks that move nothing: hardware.ball_view runs the detector on live frames, and hardware.temps shows motor temperatures and fault flags. You can also try the controller without an arm: uv run python -m sim.view_controller --rand.

Safety: the arms move on their own and stay powered after the scripts exit. Keep clear and be ready to press Ctrl+C. To release an arm, hold it and run hardware.torque_off.

Limits and next steps

  • Retrain for this robot. A controller and estimator trained with my paddle's mass and the Innomaker camera's field of view should remove the right arm's 2–3 cm offset. The simulated camera is still zecice's (62° vertical field of view).
  • Tilt test. The built-in tilt test (--tilt-test) was inconclusive: with a 3° tilt, the leftover level error dominated. Balancing works, so the joint signs are right.
  • Orange balls. They would make the detector trivial again.
