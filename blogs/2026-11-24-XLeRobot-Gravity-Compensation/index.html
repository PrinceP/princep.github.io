Two robot arms holding a board level, soft to the touch

Gravity compensation, impedance control and a two-arm compliant board hold on the XLeRobot's SO-101 arms. The arms use hobby-grade Feetech STS3215 servos, which have no torque mode. The control loop is written in Rust and runs on a Jetson Orin Nano. It's a port of NVIDIA's "Make a robot arm feel weightless with Holoscan" to much cheaper hardware.

The robot

Everything here runs on my MakerMods XLeRobot build. That's MakerMods' upgraded hardware for the XLeRobot bimanual mobile robot: a redesigned 3D-printed structure on an IKEA RÅSKOG cart, with an omni-wheel base. It's drop-in compatible with LeRobot. The compute is an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano (JetPack 6, L4T R36.5) mounted on the robot.

Can LeRobot do this?

NVIDIA's version uses a reBot arm with quasi-direct-drive motors that accept torque commands, and runs a Holoscan graph at 400 Hz. The XLeRobot is very different hardware, so the first question was whether the idea carries over at all. In short: yes, approximately.

How it works

Each arm runs its own thread on its own serial bus. Every 4 ms the thread reads all joints in one transaction, works out the torque each joint needs, converts it to PWM, and writes all the PWM values in one reply-less transaction.

Mapping to NVIDIA's operators: JointStateSourceOp is the sync read, PinocchioGravityOp is ArmModel::gravity plus the per-joint PWM gains, and MotorCommandSinkOp is the PWM write. Their watchdog becomes "every exit path holds the arm in position mode".

pwm = pwm_sign · ( pwm_per_nm · [ g(q) + Jᵀ(K·e − D·v) ] + friction ) + virtual walls near the joint limits q_urdf = sign · deg2rad((raw − mid) · 360/4095) + offset (LeRobot degrees → URDF angles)

Gravity compensation

The PWM sign, the PWM-per-N·m gain and the static friction of every joint were measured on the robot with gravcomp identify. It ramps PWM against gravity until the joint moves 0.7°, then back until it moves the other way. The joint travels about a degree in total. With all five joints compensated and nobody touching the arm, it held within 0.2° for 10 s.

The datasheet suggested about 340 PWM per N·m. The measured gains are about half that, and the two arms differ by up to 20%. That's enough to tilt a shared board, so each arm uses its own values. pwm_sign is −1 on every joint: positive PWM decreases the raw position.

Impedance control

This ports the tutorial's second example. A goal pulls the gripper toward it like a spring while gravity compensation keeps running, so you can still push the arm aside by hand. Goals arrive as JSON over UDP (left arm on port 5701, right on 5702): either joint angles, the same format an ACT-style LeRobot policy outputs, or a gripper-tip pose. The arm streams its state back at 50 Hz.

joint goal: τ = Kq (q_d − q) − Dq q̇ pose goal: τ = Jᵀ [ Kp (p_d − p) − Dp v ; Ko log(R_d Rᵀ) − Do ω ] − Dq q̇ (tool = gripper tip, arm base frame)

The goal moves toward a new target at a limited speed and is clamped to the arm's reach, and the spring force is capped. A goal that isn't refreshed expires, and the arm falls back to plain gravity compensation. So a policy that crashes leaves a soft arm, not a stiff one.

The fix for large pushes was a friction assist. While a joint is still, part of its measured break-away friction is added in the direction the spring pulls, so the spring alone doesn't have to break through friction. It fades out near the goal.

Two-arm compliant board hold

Both grippers clamp the 40 cm edge of a 40 × 30 × 1.4 cm foam board, about 6 cm in from each corner. The jaws close vertically, so the board lies flat between them. Each arm holds its gripper's position and orientation, gravity compensated and compliant. Three things made it work:

  • Level first, in position mode. The compliant hold can't make 2° corrections: turning the wrist also moves the tip, so shoulder, elbow and wrist would all have to move together, and all three are held by friction. Instead, before compliance starts, a Newton step on the model levels each gripper using the servos' own stiff position loop, to within 0.13°.
  • Equalize the tip heights (tools/level_board.py --apply) so the board is level side to side. Without a board in the grippers: 0.46° → 0.31°.
  • Soft push, firm return. One fixed setting can't be both easy to push and accurate on return, because the same friction resists both. So the friction assist stays low (0.2) while the board is moving, and ramps to 0.9 once it has been still away from its goal for 0.5 s.

Hardware lessons

Things the STS3215 servos (firmware 3.10) do that the documentation doesn't warn you about, found on the robot and handled in src/servo.rs:

  • In PWM mode, the position reading drops the homing offset. It jumped by +1164 steps on a wrist whose offset is 1163, and the change can lag the mode switch. A goal computed from one of those readings once swung a shoulder about 71° into the other arm. Now the shift is measured across the mode switch and subtracted, and a goal that disagrees with the computed one is never written.
  • Switching mode with torque off drops a loaded joint, and the motor then stopped answering. Modes are now switched with torque on, without touching the EEPROM lock, so a power cycle always comes back in position mode.
  • A single corrupted reply once read 62 °C on a 35 °C motor and stopped a run. Temperature and voltage faults now need 3 bad readings in a row.
  • Geared friction dominates. Static friction is about the size of gravity's pull, so a soft spring has a dead band of several millimetres. Accuracy against compliance is the central trade-off, and the friction assist and "soft push, firm return" are the tools for it.

Run it

From the laptop. The Makefile drives the Orin over ssh and builds the Rust binary on the Orin itself.

make test check-model                   # unit tests, plus the Rust model against Pinocchio
make rust-orin build                    # once: user-level rustup on the Orin; then sync and build
make probe                              # read-only: joint angles, model torque, load
make identify ARM=left JOINT=elbow_flex # measure PWM sign, gain and friction for one joint
make run ARM=left                       # gravity compensation (Ctrl+C holds the arm)
make imp ARM=left HOLD=1 POSE=extended  # + impedance hold; goals: tools/send_goal.py
make board-demo                         # two-arm board hold + 3-camera recording with tilt overlay
make rest ARM=both                      # fold to rest, torque off

The repository layout: src/servo.rs (STS3215 PWM-mode access), src/model.rs (URDF gravity, FK, Jacobian), src/control.rs (the loop, safety, grasp, identification, levelling), src/impedance.rs (springs and the UDP goal/state interface), config/xlerobot.toml (all gains and limits), and tools/ (Pinocchio check, goal client, board levelling, camera recorder, this site's data).

Limits and next steps

  • Orientation: hold only the two rotations a 5-joint arm can reach, so they stop competing with position.
  • Exact return: a slow, capped integral term would remove the last few millimetres; the left arm's 5 mm is the case to fix.
  • Left wrist camera: it opened but delivered no frames during the recording. The likely suspect is USB bandwidth with three MJPG streams.
  • Holoscan: on JetPack 7, the three NVIDIA operators map one-to-one onto the sync read, the gravity model and the PWM write. The serial bus would still cap the rate at about 300–400 Hz.
